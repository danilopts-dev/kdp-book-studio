# Introduction — Fifteen Minutes, One Room at a Time

If you picked up this book, there is probably a closet or a whole room you have been avoiding for a while. Maybe you have tried a weekend blitz before, pulled everything out onto the bed, and ended up at midnight shoving half of it back in because you ran out of energy before you ran out of stuff. That experience is common enough that this book is built around the opposite approach: small amounts of time, spread over twelve weeks, one room after another.

Here is the whole promise. For 12 weeks, you spend 15 minutes a day making decisions about the things in your home, and by the end you will have gone through every room at least once. You will also know what left the house, how much money came back from what you sold, and what you gave away, with the value written down in case you want it for your records. That is all this book asks of you, and it is all it claims to deliver.

What it will not do is teach you to organize. There are no labeling systems here and no drawer dividers, and I will never tell you to go buy a set of matching bins. The bags and boxes you already have in the house are enough, and Week 0 lists exactly what to gather before you start.

## Why deciding comes first

Most clutter advice starts in the wrong place, in my opinion. It shows you a beautiful shelf of clear containers and suggests that the problem is a storage problem. For most homes it is a decision problem, because every item you have not decided about is still taking up space, whether it sits in a pile on the counter or in a pretty basket on a shelf. Organizing things you do not want just gives you tidier clutter.

So the work in these pages is almost entirely deciding. You pick up an object, you decide what happens to it, and you put it in the right place for that decision. Once most of the extra is gone, organizing what is left tends to take care of itself, because there is simply less to arrange. If you still want bins at the end of Week 12, buy them then, when you know exactly what is going inside.

## The four boxes

Every item you touch goes into one of four boxes: **Keep**, **Donate**, **Sell** or **Toss**. They can be cardboard boxes or heavy-duty trash bags with a strip of tape on each one. What matters is that there are four, and that they are there before you open the first drawer.

- **Keep** means it goes back into the room, in a place where you can find it. Keep does not mean "set it aside for later."
- **Donate** is for things in good enough shape that someone else would use them.
- **Sell** is for things worth the time it takes to list them and meet a buyer.
- **Toss** is for anything broken, expired, stained or missing its parts. Recycle what you can.

At the end of each week there is a page called "Keep / Donate / Sell / Toss Count," where you tally what went where. It looks like bookkeeping, and it is, but after a few weeks those numbers show you plainly that the house is changing.

### When you can't decide

You will hit items that do not fit neatly anywhere. A sweater you like but never wear, or the charger for a phone you gave up years ago. For those, use one question as the tie-breaker:

**If this were gone tomorrow, would I go out and buy it again?**

If the honest answer is yes, it is a Keep. If the answer is no, or "I'd have to think about it," it leaves the house. From there, the only question is whether it goes to Donate or Sell, and that depends on money and patience. A good rule is to pick a dollar amount below which selling is not worth your time. For some people that is $20, for others it is $50 or more. Write your number down in Week 0 and stick to it, so you are not debating every single item.

You will notice there is no "maybe" box. I left it out on purpose, since a maybe box is usually just the old clutter moved into a new container. If you truly cannot answer the question today, it is a Keep for now, and you will meet it again when you do a 15-minute reset later in the book.

## Why the rooms go in this order

The weeks do not follow the layout of a house. They follow difficulty, starting with the rooms where decisions are quick and moving toward the ones where they are slow and sometimes painful.

Week 1 is the bathroom, because almost everything in there has an expiration date or a clear use, and most people can fill a Toss bag in a single session without feeling guilty about any of it. That first quick result is the whole reason it comes first. After that you move through the entryway, the kitchen (split over two weeks, since it holds more than most people expect), the living room and your bedroom. The closet has its own week because clothes are usually the biggest pile and the easiest to sell. Then come the kids' rooms or a hobby room, the paper pile, the linen and laundry closets, and finally the garage, basement and attic, where the volume is large and the jobs have to be broken into 15-minute pieces.

Sentimental things wait until Week 12. By then you will have made a few hundred small decisions, and you will know how your own hesitation feels and what usually settles it. You will also have more room in the house, which makes it easier to keep the few things that truly matter where you can see them. Letters, photos, a parent's watch and your kids' drawings deserve that kind of attention, and they get a full week with pages to photograph an item and write down its story before you decide what to do with the object itself. Some people find that week easy after everything else, while others still find it hard, and that is fine too.

## How to use the pages

Each week opens with a short page about the room, followed by "This Week, 15 Minutes a Day," which gives you seven small tasks, one per day, with a box to check. After that come the pages specific to that room, and the week closes with your count and a space for notes and wins. You can skip a day. You can also take two weeks for a room if your kitchen is bigger than the plan assumes, since nobody is grading you on the calendar.

Part 3 is where you keep track of everything that went out of the house, with a Sell Tracker and a Donation Log. Part 4 helps you keep the house from filling up again, and when the decluttering is done, the *House Cleaning Checklist Planner* picks up the routine for keeping it clean.

Before you start, turn to the Printable Declutter Kit page at the back of this book. It tells you how to get the kit, which is handy if you would rather print fresh sheets than write in the book, or if more than one person in the house wants to follow along.

Then go to Week 0, walk through your home with a pencil, and don't touch anything yet.
